THE TRAVELER: CALCULATING PERSONAL COORDINATES

By Barstow Bill Tonnesen | Drafted at Wrightwood Station (92397)

I. The Chalkboard Canon

Every operating system relies on a reference manual. While Sunday congregations pass around a pre-written book demanding uncritical compliance, my canon was written in chalk across Southern California. It began with the foundational arithmetic of Mrs. Hand at La Quinta, took spatial form through the geometry of Mr. Johnson at Colton, and reached full dynamic motion under the calculus of Mr. Miller at Fullerton and Mr. Kelly at Palomar.

Those four instructors handed me an empirical baseline: you prove a structure before you trust it with a load. On a structural steel drawing at EDS or SMI-Joist, if a chord length or miter angle is off by a fraction of an inch, the iron refuses to bolt together. Reality does not bend to dogma.

II. Load-Bearing Loyalty vs. Tactical Extraction

Understanding structural load also defines what you walk away from and what you stay to carry. Extracting from Chang's sweatshop in Carlsbad in 1988—or executing the Hesperia exit decades later—was simple mechanical math: when an external system is rigged to fail, you pack your tools and move. Yet in 1996, when Devorah looked at the fractures in my marriage and advised me to leave Lydia, I couldn't and wouldn't. Human loyalty isn't a disposable contract; when the bond is personal, you plant your feet and bear the weight.

III. Brooklyn Gold Leaf and the Peterbilt Wheel

When Sandra tried to pull me into her Korean "Mother God" faction—using the exact same Bible as the Sunday Kingdom Hall to arrive at a completely contradictory reality—the mathematical proof failed on sight. If two operators run the same equation and produce opposite coordinates, the system is compromised.

It was my younger brother Mike who cut straight through the noise: "Forget Sandra." Mike remained a devoted Jehovah's Witness to the very end of his life, leaving instructions with his congregation: "Keep my harmonica around for when I return." In his youth, Mike stood inside the Watchtower plant in Brooklyn, literally applying the gold leaf to the edges of those Bibles. Meanwhile, across the country, my weathered hands were muscling a loaded hand truck and gripping the steering wheel of a Peterbilt, calculating real-world payload physics on mountain grades.

Mike knew the machinery of religion from the factory floor up, and he looked across the divide and called me The Expert. He knew my coordinates were already locked in.
